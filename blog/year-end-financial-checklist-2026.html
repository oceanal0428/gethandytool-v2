Year-End Financial Checklist: 12 Moves Before December 31, 2026

The last eight weeks of the year are when most tax-advantaged financial moves still count. Here are 12 concrete actions — ranked by deadline urgency — that can measurably affect your 2026 tax bill, retirement balance, and financial setup for 2027.

Most year-end checklists read like a reminder to "think about your finances." This one is a sequenced action list with the actual deadlines. Items near the top are strictly year-end (December 31, 2026). Items near the bottom give you a little more runway into early 2027 — but doing them now beats January scrambling.

Deadline Tier 1: Must Do Before December 31

1. Max Out Your 401(k) Contribution (If You Can)

Deadline: Your last 2026 paycheck

401(k) contributions have a strict calendar-year deadline tied to your payroll. Any dollar you want deducted from 2026 taxable income must come out of a paycheck issued before December 31. Catch-up contributions apply if you're 50+. If you're on track but have room, increase your contribution percentage for the final paychecks of the year to hit the limit.

Check your current year-to-date contribution on your most recent pay stub, compare it to the IRS annual limit, and adjust. Many payroll systems allow last-minute percentage changes; others require a few weeks of lead time, so don't wait until the 29th.

2. Use Your FSA Balance

Deadline: December 31, 2026 (with possible grace period or $640 carryover depending on your employer's plan)

Flexible Spending Account dollars are "use it or lose it." Some employers offer a grace period until March 15 or allow up to $640 to carry over; many do neither. Check your employer's specific rule now.

Eligible spend typically includes: prescription glasses and contact lenses, orthotics, allergy medications, thermometers, first aid supplies, menstrual care products, sunscreen (SPF 15+), prescription medications, and many over-the-counter items. Dental checkups, eye exams, and planned procedures can also apply.

3. Take Required Minimum Distributions (RMDs)

Deadline: December 31, 2026 (first RMD only: April 1, 2027)

If you're 73 or older (or inherited an IRA with RMD requirements), the annual RMD must be withdrawn by December 31 to avoid a 25% penalty on the shortfall. If 2026 is your first RMD year, you have until April 1, 2027 — but doubling up in one year can push you into a higher tax bracket, so most people prefer to take the first RMD in the original year.

Many brokerages have RMD calculators built into their platforms; use them to verify the amount before you request the distribution. Consider directing the RMD to a qualified charity via a QCD (Qualified Charitable Distribution) to satisfy the RMD without counting the distribution as taxable income — one of the most tax-efficient moves available to charitable retirees.

4. Harvest Tax Losses (and Gains Strategically)

Deadline: December 31, 2026 for the trade to count toward the 2026 tax year

Review your taxable brokerage account for positions sitting at a loss. Selling realizes the loss, which can offset capital gains (unlimited) plus up to $3,000 of ordinary income. Excess losses carry forward indefinitely.

Watch the wash sale rule: you can't claim the loss if you repurchase the same or a "substantially identical" security within 30 days before or after the sale. To stay invested in the sector, switch to a different but similar ETF (e.g., sell VTI, buy ITOT).

Conversely, if you're in a low-income year (temporary job loss, sabbatical, early retirement), consider realizing long-term gains at the 0% capital gains rate (available for single filers under about $47,000 taxable income or married-filing-jointly under about $94,000 in 2026).

5. Finalize Charitable Donations

Deadline: December 31, 2026 (postmarked by that date for mail checks)

Cash donations, appreciated stock, QCDs, and donor-advised fund contributions all count toward the tax year in which they're made. If you itemize, donations reduce taxable income — but the standard deduction ($15,000 single / $30,000 married in 2026) means most filers don't itemize. Bundling multiple years of donations into one year (via donor-advised funds) can push you above the standard deduction threshold in alternating years.

Donating appreciated stock held over a year is particularly efficient: you get the full fair-market-value deduction without paying capital gains tax on the appreciation. For public-company shares with large unrealized gains, this often beats donating cash.

Deadline Tier 2: Early January 2027 Still Works

6. Make IRA Contributions

Deadline: April 15, 2027 (for 2026 contribution year)

Unlike 401(k)s, IRA contributions for the 2026 tax year can be made until the federal tax filing deadline. The current contribution limit is $7,000 ($8,000 if 50+). Roth IRA eligibility phases out at higher incomes; Traditional IRA deductibility phases out if you're covered by a workplace retirement plan.

If you're above the Roth IRA income limit, consider the "backdoor Roth" strategy: contribute to a Traditional IRA, then convert to Roth. Works cleanly only if you don't have other pre-tax IRA balances (pro-rata rule applies).

7. HSA Contributions

Deadline: April 15, 2027

Health Savings Accounts have the same filing-deadline contribution window as IRAs. HSAs are the most tax-advantaged account available — contributions are deductible, growth is tax-free, and withdrawals for qualified medical expenses are tax-free. After age 65, non-medical withdrawals are taxed only as ordinary income (like a Traditional IRA).

2026 HSA contribution limits are $4,300 (self-only HDHP) and $8,550 (family HDHP), plus $1,000 catch-up if 55+. If you haven't maxed yours and you're on an HSA-eligible health plan, this is almost always worth doing.

Deadline Tier 3: Setup for 2027

8. Rebalance Your Investment Portfolio

Timing: Anytime, but year-end is a natural rebalancing checkpoint

After strong market years, your stock allocation drifts above target. After weak years, bonds carry too much. Rebalance back to your target allocation. In tax-advantaged accounts (401(k), IRA), rebalancing has no tax cost. In taxable accounts, prefer rebalancing by directing new contributions rather than selling appreciated positions.

9. Update Beneficiaries on All Accounts

Timing: Immediate, but at least annually

Beneficiary designations on retirement accounts, life insurance, and payable-on-death accounts override whatever your will says. Life events in 2026 — marriage, divorce, new child, death of a listed beneficiary — frequently leave accounts pointing to the wrong person. Check all your 401(k), IRA, HSA, insurance, and bank POD designations this month.

10. Reassess Health Insurance for 2027

Open enrollment: Varies by employer; ACA Marketplace runs November 1 – January 15

Open enrollment is often the only time all year you can change health plans without a qualifying life event. Review next year's premiums, deductibles, and network changes. If you had meaningful medical expenses in 2026, consider whether switching from a high-deductible plan to a lower-deductible plan would have paid off (or vice versa for a healthy year).

If you're on an ACA Marketplace plan, recalculate your expected 2027 income — subsidies depend on it. Underestimating income means paying back subsidies at tax time; overestimating means leaving money on the table.

11. Review and Cancel Unused Subscriptions

Timing: This month

Recurring subscriptions silently accumulate over the year. Average Americans carry $200+ monthly in subscriptions they rarely use. Scan last 12 months of credit card statements for every recurring charge. Keep what you actually use; cancel the rest. A 30-minute audit in December typically saves several hundred dollars annually.

12. Document Your 2026 Financial Picture

Timing: December 31, 2026 snapshot

Take a one-day snapshot of all account balances, total debt, retirement balances, home value, and vehicle values on December 31. Save it to a spreadsheet or document. One year from now, you'll have a clean year-over-year comparison that reveals whether the year's financial efforts actually moved the needle.

Many financial setbacks go unnoticed for years because nobody kept clean baseline numbers. One snapshot per year is enough.

Common Year-End Mistakes to Avoid

Rushing Tax Decisions in Late December

Charitable donations, Roth conversions, and large Section 529 contributions all have 12/31 deadlines, which tempts people into decisions without sufficient analysis. If you're considering a $50,000 Roth conversion in late December, run the actual tax math with your CPA or in tax software — a bad conversion can cost thousands in unnecessary taxes.

Over-Contributing to Retirement Accounts

Hitting contribution limits across multiple employers (job change mid-year) or across workplace + IRA accounts without checking limits creates excess contributions. The IRS charges 6% annually on excess amounts until withdrawn. Confirm year-to-date totals before the last paycheck of the year.

Ignoring State Tax Rules

Several states have their own year-end nuances — state tax deductions for 529 contributions (deadline often 12/31), state-level charitable deduction limits, or special state retirement contributions. Don't optimize federal moves while ignoring state tax implications.

Forgetting to Adjust 2027 Withholding

If you had a large tax refund in 2026, you're over-withholding — essentially giving the IRS an interest-free loan. Adjust your W-4 for 2027. If you owed a large balance, increase withholding to avoid underpayment penalties. The IRS withholding estimator at irs.gov is free and accurate.

The Honest Bottom Line

Year-end financial checklists feel overwhelming because many people try to do all of this in the last week of December. Spread across November and early December, each item takes 15–30 minutes. By December 15, you can have everything substantial done and enjoy the holidays without a tax-cliff hanging over them.

Start with Tier 1 items this week — those are the ones that genuinely vanish at year-end. Tier 2 and 3 can wait until after Thanksgiving. If you only have time for three items: max your 401(k), take any RMDs, and spend down your FSA.
