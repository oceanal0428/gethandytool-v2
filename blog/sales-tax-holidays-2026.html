Sales Tax Holidays 2026: Complete State-by-State Schedule

Roughly 17 states run sales tax holidays — limited-time exemptions for specific categories like back-to-school items, hurricane preparedness, energy-efficient appliances, and firearms. Strategic shoppers can save 5–10% on large purchases by timing them right.

A sales tax holiday is a state-designated period (usually 2–3 days, occasionally a week) during which certain items can be purchased without state — and sometimes local — sales tax. They're typically targeted at specific categories tied to public-interest goals: helping families afford school supplies, preparing for hurricane season, encouraging energy efficiency, or supporting specific industries.

The savings can be meaningful. On a $1,500 back-to-school shopping trip in a state with a 7% sales tax, a tax holiday saves $105. On a $3,000 set of hurricane-preparedness supplies including generators and shutters, $210. Plan ahead and you can collapse months of intended spending into tax-free windows.

This guide covers the major 2026 sales tax holidays by state, the items that typically qualify, the common price caps, and a practical strategy for maximum benefit.

How Sales Tax Holidays Work

Each state sets its own rules. The general framework:

  • Specific categories: Only items in defined categories qualify (e.g., "clothing under $100"). Items outside the category remain taxable.
  • Price caps per item: Most holidays cap the per-item price ($100 for clothing, $1,500 for computers, etc.). An item priced above the cap is fully taxable, not partially.
  • State + sometimes local tax: In some states, local taxes are also waived during the holiday. In others, only the state portion is exempt while local taxes still apply.
  • Online purchases: Many states extend the exemption to online purchases shipped to addresses in the state during the holiday window. Verify with the retailer that the discount will apply at checkout.
  • No need to apply: The exemption is applied automatically at point of sale. Retailers handle the calculation.

Back-to-School Tax Holidays (Most Common Type)

These are the most widespread holidays, typically held in late July or early August, covering clothing, school supplies, and (in some states) computers. Specific dates vary year to year — always verify with your state's revenue department a few weeks before shopping.

Common Qualifying Items

  • Clothing and footwear (per-item caps typically $75–$125)
  • School supplies (notebooks, pens, paper, calculators, etc.)
  • Backpacks
  • Computers and tablets (in some states, caps of $750–$1,500)
  • Educational software (less common)
  • Books and printed instructional materials (rare)

Hurricane Preparedness Tax Holidays

States along the Atlantic and Gulf coasts run these in late spring or early summer, ahead of hurricane season. Items typically include:

  • Portable generators (caps usually $1,000–$3,000)
  • Storm shutters and tarps
  • Batteries and battery-powered radios
  • Flashlights
  • Food storage containers
  • Coolers and ice packs
  • First aid supplies
  • Self-powered chargers
  • Carbon monoxide detectors and smoke alarms

States with hurricane preparedness holidays typically include Alabama, Florida, Louisiana, Texas, and Virginia. Florida's holiday in particular is broad — covering many items not strictly tied to hurricane preparation.

Energy-Efficient Appliance Holidays

Several states run holidays for ENERGY STAR-certified appliances and water-efficient products. Items can include:

  • Refrigerators and freezers
  • Dishwashers
  • Washing machines and dryers
  • Air conditioners
  • Water heaters
  • Programmable thermostats
  • LED light bulbs

Caps vary: Texas exempts appliances under $6,000; Virginia has broader rules. Georgia, Missouri, and others have run similar holidays in different years.

Firearms and Hunting Holidays

Several states have sales tax holidays for firearms, ammunition, and hunting supplies, typically in late summer or fall. Mississippi, Louisiana, Tennessee, and West Virginia have all run versions. Categories typically include rifles, shotguns, ammunition, archery equipment, and hunting accessories.

These holidays are politically contentious in some states and may be modified or eliminated over time. Check current state rules.

Other State-Specific Holidays

Some states have unique holidays for niche categories:

  • Florida Freedom Week: Broad outdoor recreation items in early July
  • Texas Water-Efficient Products Sales Tax Holiday: Late spring, for irrigation and water-saving items
  • Maryland Energy Star Weekend: February

Strategy: How to Actually Save Money

1. Plan Large Purchases Around Holiday Dates

If you'd buy something within a month or two of the holiday anyway, shift the purchase to the tax-free window. A $1,500 laptop bought during Florida's holiday saves about $90 — more than enough to make the planning worthwhile.

2. Stay Within the Per-Item Caps

Most holidays exempt items up to a price cap. An item priced just over the cap is fully taxable, not partially. A $101 pair of shoes in a state with a $100 cap is fully taxable. Choose the $99 version (or shop multiple items separately) to qualify.

3. Verify Online Eligibility

Major retailers (Amazon, Target, Walmart) handle tax holidays automatically for items shipped to addresses in qualifying states. Smaller online retailers may not. If buying online during a holiday, confirm at checkout that sales tax has been waived. If not, you may need to make the purchase in-store.

4. Combine With Other Sales

Major retailers often run their own sales during tax holiday weekends to attract additional foot traffic. Stacking a 20% sale with tax holiday savings means a 25%+ effective discount on qualifying items.

5. Beware "Tax Holiday" Pricing Inflation

Some retailers raise prices in the weeks before a tax holiday and run "sales" that bring them back to normal. The "discount" is illusory. Track prices for 4–6 weeks before the holiday using browser extensions or price-tracking sites to ensure you're getting genuine savings.

6. Don't Buy What You Don't Need

The most common tax holiday trap is buying things you wouldn't otherwise — "it's tax-free!" If you save 7% on something you didn't need, you've still spent 93% you didn't need to. Tax holidays save you money only on planned purchases.

Categories That Are Always Taxable

Even during tax holidays, certain items are typically NOT exempt:

  • Alcohol, tobacco, candy, soda
  • Restaurant meals and prepared food
  • Cosmetics, jewelry, watches
  • Sporting equipment (in most states, with exceptions for hunting/water sports during specific holidays)
  • Furniture (except appliance holidays)
  • Vehicles
  • Rentals and services

States With No Sales Tax Holidays

Several states never run sales tax holidays:

  • States with no general sales tax (Oregon, New Hampshire, Montana, Delaware, Alaska state-level)
  • States that explicitly opted out (most of the Northeast and West Coast)
  • States that have ended past holidays due to budget or effectiveness concerns (Georgia, North Carolina)

Residents of these states can cross borders if a neighboring state is running a holiday. Use tax considerations may technically apply — but for typical consumer purchases, enforcement is minimal.

How to Stay Updated

Sales tax holiday dates and rules change annually. The most reliable sources:

  • Your state's Department of Revenue website — search "[state name] sales tax holiday 2026"
  • The Federation of Tax Administrators publishes a consolidated annual list
  • Major retailers announce their participation a few weeks ahead

State legislatures sometimes add or modify holidays mid-year. Confirm before committing to large purchases.

The Honest Bottom Line

Sales tax holidays are a legitimate way to save 5%–10% on purchases you'd make anyway. They work best for planned spending — back-to-school clothing, holiday-season appliances, hurricane preparation — that can be deliberately timed.

They don't work as well for unplanned spending or for items you wouldn't otherwise buy. "I saved 7%!" on something unnecessary just means you spent 93% unnecessarily.

The savings are typically modest in dollar terms ($50–$200 per major shopping trip), but compounded across the year for a family, they add up. Combine them with retailer sales and price-tracking, and you can stretch your back-to-school or appliance budget meaningfully.
